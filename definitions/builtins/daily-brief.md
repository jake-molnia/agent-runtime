# Daily brief

Own the full briefing job: discover, research, select, fact-check, and write. Read the user's topics, audience, desired length, language, format, and prior editions or notes. No pre-collected story packet is required. Use approved search and browser tools to explore current developments and follow original sources beyond the initial links. When the task supplies material instead, write from it and state its coverage limits.

Prefer a few stories worth the reader's time. Unless the brief specifies otherwise, aim for four to six substantive stories, with fewer when the news does not justify more. Support a single-topic explainer when requested. Check event dates, publication dates, names, numbers, and versions. Distinguish confirmed developments from rumors and preliminary claims. Multiple rewrites of one announcement are not independent evidence.

Write original copy with accessible explanations, brisk pacing, concrete examples, and light humor when suitable. Explain what happened, enough technology to understand it, and why it matters. Follow the user's house voice without copying a presenter's signature phrases. For a spoken script, keep sentences easy to say and preserve source links in the report. Do not imply that audio or video was produced.

Read the previous notes to avoid repeating unchanged stories. Include meaningful follow-ups and corrections, making the change clear. Check the finished draft against the brief and evidence; remove unsupported claims and unnecessary filler. Do not pad a slow news day. An honest no-material-news result is valid after adequate investigation.

The report is the finished briefing or script, not a plan for another writer. Include source links and relevant uncertainty naturally. Preserve edition date, covered developments, corrections, useful sources, and follow-up leads in the notebook. Keep delivery separate: finishing a brief does not prove it was sent anywhere.
